\begin{abstract}
Deep neural networks now run on commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) processors aboard Earth-observation satellites, where
single-event upsets (SEUs) from trapped protons silently corrupt the stored weights. In IEEE-754 single precision, one
flip of the most significant exponent bit turns a weight of order one into a value near $10^{38}$, collapsing a
classifier to chance. Existing remedies sit at two extremes: blind periodic scrubbing reloads the entire model on a
timer, and in-situ software mitigation (activation clipping, batch-norm re-centering) leaves the corrupted weights in
place. We present \aegis{}, a detection-gated, layer-selective repair loop. A label-free behavioral monitor (an
activation-range envelope and a batch-norm drift test) proposes suspect blocks on each incoming image batch; a CRC-32
comparison against golden checksums confirms which blocks actually changed, including the block just upstream of the
earliest alarm; only confirmed blocks are restored, bit-exactly, from a compressed golden copy in radiation-tolerant
storage. An optional periodic CRC audit catches faults too small to change behavior. We evaluate \aegis{} in a
paired, seeded mission simulator that flies a real Sentinel-2A track (SGP4), defines the South Atlantic Anomaly
physically from IGRF field strength, and calibrates upset rates to eight years of Alsat-1 flight data
($4.04\times10^{-7}$ upsets/bit/day). Over three simulated days and three radiation seeds on EuroSAT, \aegis{} keeps a
ResNet-18 at $87.4\%$ mean accuracy (clean $89.3\%$) versus $37.0\%$ for 12-hour scrubbing, $38.5\%$ for range
clipping and $14.0\%$ unprotected, winning every paired seed; matching its accuracy by scrubbing would take
$4.7\times$ the repair traffic. A held-out-trained MobileNetV2 shows the same ordering at $3.4$\,MB/day. We also report
a negative result: always-on monitoring makes \aegis{} cost $5$--$22\times$ more energy than slow scrubbing under our
energy model, so its advantage is bandwidth and availability, not energy. We document the evaluation pitfalls that
invalidated our own early results and release the simulator, campaigns and an interactive dossier.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{O}{nboard} inference has moved from demonstration to routine. $\Phi$-Sat-1 flew a convolutional cloud
screener on a COTS vision accelerator in 2020 and discarded cloudy frames before downlink~\cite{giuffrida2022phisat};
OPS-SAT trained and replaced models in orbit~\cite{opssat}; and current small-satellite payloads carry
Jetson-class GPUs and neural accelerators~\cite{furano2020edge,rednet2024}. The appeal is plain: the downlink is the
bottleneck of Earth observation, so deciding onboard what is worth sending multiplies the useful data a satellite
returns. The cost is reliability. COTS processors and memories are not radiation hardened, and in low Earth orbit
(LEO) energetic protons trapped in the inner Van Allen belt, concentrated over the South Atlantic Anomaly
(SAA)~\cite{heirtzler2002saa}, deposit enough charge to flip stored bits~\cite{baumann2005soft,petersen2011see}.

A neural network is an unusually fragile thing to store in such memory. Its knowledge lives in millions of
floating-point weights, and the IEEE-754 binary32 format~\cite{ieee754} packs an 8-bit exponent beside the sign.
Trained weights are small, so their exponent's most significant bit is almost always zero; flipping it multiplies the
weight by $2^{128}\approx3.4\times10^{38}$. One such upset in a convolution kernel saturates every activation
downstream and drives the classifier to chance, as targeted bit-flip attacks showed on purpose~\cite{rakin2019bfa}.
Upsets accumulate: unlike a transient glitch in logic, a flipped bit in SRAM or DRAM stays flipped until something
rewrites it.

Spacecraft engineering answers with hardware: error-detecting and correcting codes (EDAC), triple modular redundancy
and memory scrubbing, the periodic rewriting of memory from a trusted copy~\cite{petersen2011see,bentoutou2011edac}.
Scrubbing is simple and robust but blind: it pays to rewrite the whole model whether or not anything is wrong, and
between scrubs the model runs corrupted. The machine-learning community answers with software. Range restriction
(Ranger~\cite{chen2021ranger}, FT-ClipAct~\cite{hoang2020ftclipact}) clamps activations to profiled bounds;
BLINK~\cite{blink2026} detects batch-norm drift and re-centers activations; RADAR~\cite{li2021radar} and
MILR~\cite{ponader2021milr} detect or algebraically recover corrupted weights. These methods mitigate symptoms in
place but, with the exception of algebraic recovery, leave the corrupted weights in memory, where further upsets
compound them. A third option is to wait for a ground station and re-upload the model, which costs scarce uplink time
and leaves the model broken until the next contact.

This paper asks whether a satellite can do better by combining the two worlds: use cheap, label-free signals from the
network's own activity to notice damage, use exact checksums to prove which stored blocks changed, and then restore
only those blocks from an onboard golden copy. We call this \emph{detection-gated, layer-selective restore} and build
it as \aegis{}. The idea is simple; demonstrating it honestly is not. Evaluations of DNN resilience are easy to get
wrong in ways that flatter the method: unrealistic fault models (zeroed weights instead of exponent
flips)~\cite{dossantos2023neutron}, synthetic inputs, monitors whose thresholds fire on every clean batch, energy
budgets that omit the monitor itself. Our own first prototype reported a $98.9\%$ energy saving that did not survive
correction (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}). We therefore treat the evaluation methodology as a first-class contribution.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A detection-gated, layer-selective repair loop} (\aegis{}) that combines a two-tier label-free
  behavioral monitor with CRC-32 confirmation, including an \emph{upstream-block rule} that localizes batch-norm
  parameter faults whose symptoms first appear one block downstream, and an optional periodic CRC audit for
  sub-threshold faults (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{A paired, orbit-calibrated mission simulator.} Faults are placed uniformly over stored bits at rates
  integrated minute by minute along a real Sentinel-2A track, with the SAA defined physically from IGRF field strength
  and the orbit-averaged rate calibrated to Alsat-1 flight data. Every strategy replays the identical fault stream, so
  comparisons are paired (Section~\ref{sec:faultmodel}).
  \item \textbf{A head-to-head evaluation} against unprotected inference, range clipping, BN re-centering, periodic
  scrubbing at five intervals, and a ground-uplink reload, on two architectures with 95\% intervals over seeds,
  measuring accuracy over time, repair traffic and modeled energy (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{An honest cost accounting with a negative result}: once the monitor's compute is charged, \aegis{} is
  not an energy saver; it wins on bandwidth and availability (Section~\ref{sec:costs}).
  \item \textbf{A catalogue of evaluation pitfalls}, each of which silently invalidated a result during this work,
  with the invariant that prevents it (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Radiation in Low Earth Orbit}
In LEO the dominant source of SEUs in memory is the flux of trapped protons in the inner radiation belt, supplemented
by galactic cosmic rays and solar particle events~\cite{petersen2011see}. Because Earth's dipole is offset and tilted,
the inner belt dips to its lowest altitude over the South Atlantic, where the field strength at a given altitude is
weakest; this is the SAA~\cite{heirtzler2002saa}. A sun-synchronous satellite crosses it on several consecutive orbits
each day, and most memory upsets on such missions are recorded inside it. The rate also varies with the solar cycle;
on Alsat-1 the yearly mean rose from 41 to 119 upsets/day over eight years~\cite{bentoutou2011edac}.

The Alsat-1 record is unusually useful for calibration: between November 2002 and August 2010 its commercial SRAM
logged 265,649 upsets in 2,622 days, i.e.\ $4.04\times10^{-7}$ upsets/bit/day, of which $98.55\%$ were single-bit
upsets (SBUs) and the remainder multiple-bit upsets (MBUs)~\cite{bentoutou2011edac}.

A binary32 value is $v=(-1)^{s}\,2^{e-127}\,(1+f)$ with sign $s$, biased exponent $e\in[1,254]$ for normal numbers,
and fraction $f\in[0,1)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:float}). A flip in mantissa bit $k$ changes $v$ by a factor in
$[1-2^{k-23},\,1+2^{k-23}]$, so most mantissa flips are invisible to a classifier. A flip in exponent bit $j\in[23,30]$
multiplies $v$ by $2^{\pm 2^{j-23}}$. For trained weights $|v|<2$ means $e\le127$, so bit 30 is zero and a flip sets
it, multiplying by exactly $2^{128}$; if the result exceeds the format's range it becomes $\pm\infty$. A sign flip on
a batch-norm running variance makes $\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}$ undefined and yields NaN. Under uniform placement, about
one hit in 32 lands on bit 30; we recorded $4.89$ such hits per day on ResNet-18 against $4.6$ expected.

\subsection{Onboard Earth-Observation Classification}
We use land-use/land-cover classification of Sentinel-2~\cite{drusch2012sentinel2} imagery as the workload, with the
EuroSAT benchmark~\cite{helber2019eurosat}: 27,000 RGB chips of $64\times64$ pixels in ten classes (Annual Crop, Forest,
Herbaceous Vegetation, Highway, Industrial, Pasture, Permanent Crop, Residential, River, Sea/Lake). The models are a
ResNet-18~\cite{he2016resnet} and a MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}, both with batch
normalization~\cite{ioffe2015bn}, the two families most often proposed for onboard vision.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Hardware mitigation.} EDAC/ECC, selective or full TMR, and scrubbing are mature and remain the default for
flight software memory~\cite{petersen2011see,bentoutou2011edac}. ECC adapted to DNN weights has been proposed with
substantial area overhead~\cite{eccdnn2025}, and hybrid redundant accelerators target satellite AI
directly~\cite{safeneureka2026}. ZEDAC protects model parameters in memory and storage through a rad-tolerant
companion~\cite{zedac2026}. Beam experiments caution that many critical DNN errors originate in logic that ECC does not
cover, and that single-bit injection can underestimate real misdetection rates~\cite{dossantos2023neutron}. Our work is
complementary: \aegis{} assumes a protected golden copy and repairs the unprotected working copy.

\textbf{Algorithm-based fault tolerance and checksums.} ABFT~\cite{huang1984abft} and its convolutional descendants
detect errors in computation via checksums; RADAR~\cite{li2021radar} compares group checksums of weights against
golden signatures and zeroes flagged groups; weight reconstruction~\cite{li2020reconstruction} and
WeightSentry~\cite{weightsentry2025} defend against targeted bit-flip attacks, which, like radiation, are most
damaging in the exponent MSB~\cite{rakin2019bfa}. We use the simplest member of this family, CRC-32~\cite{peterson1961crc}
per block, but only as a confirmer gated by behavior.

\textbf{Range restriction.} Ranger~\cite{chen2021ranger}, FT-ClipAct~\cite{hoang2020ftclipact} and
ProAct~\cite{proact2024} clamp activations to bounds profiled on clean data. They are cheap and need no storage, but
they do not repair weights; under accumulating upsets the clipped network degrades, which our results confirm for
ResNet-18. RedNet combines such tolerance with application awareness and was validated on the Chaohu-1
satellite~\cite{rednet2024}.

\textbf{Batch-norm based detection.} BLINK~\cite{blink2026} uses batch-norm statistics as integrity checkpoints to
detect weight corruption in situ and re-centers anomalous activations. Our Tier~2 is BLINK-style, and our BLINK
baseline implements its re-centering mitigation. The difference is what happens next: \aegis{} restores weights rather
than compensating for them.

\textbf{Weight correction and self-healing.} MILR~\cite{ponader2021milr} recovers layers algebraically from stored
input/output relations; CoG-guided correction~\cite{cog2026} substitutes profiled values for detected faulty weights;
SHNN~\cite{shnn2026} localizes damage by activation discrepancy and retrains the afflicted layer, which needs labeled
data. Replacing corrupted layers from backup copies appears in patent literature~\cite{uspatent12634320}.
Plasticity methods such as ReDo~\cite{sokar2023redo} and continual backpropagation~\cite{dohare2024plasticity}
reinitialize dormant units during training; they cannot restore a specific learned function after hardware faults.

\textbf{Space ML robustness.} Lange et al.\ surveyed the robustness of onboard ML to radiation and found prior work
seldom modeled natural hazards for spacecraft models~\cite{lange2024mlspace}; SENTRY analyzed vision transformers on
EuroSAT under soft errors~\cite{sentry2026}; position papers call for radiation-aware AI runtimes~\cite{aispace2026}.
Fault injectors such as PyTorchFI~\cite{mahmoud2020pytorchfi} and Ares~\cite{reagen2018ares} established
application-level injection as a valid proxy.

\section{Fault Model and Mission Simulator}
\label{sec:faultmodel}

\subsection{What Is Corrupted}
Let the model's stored state be $\theta\in\mathbb{F}_{32}^{N}$, all parameters and batch-norm buffers, occupying
$B=32N$ bits: $358.1$\,Mbit for ResNet-18 ($44.77$\,MB) and $72.7$\,Mbit for MobileNetV2 ($9.08$\,MB). We model SEUs
in the working copy of $\theta$ held in volatile memory. Faults persist until a repair overwrites them. We do not
inject into activations, control logic or the golden copy, which we assume resides in radiation-tolerant
non-volatile memory (MRAM or rad-hard SPI NOR); transient activation faults are studied separately
(Section~\ref{sec:transient}).

\subsection{Upset Arrival Along a Real Orbit}
The satellite follows Sentinel-2A (NORAD 40697, $\approx786$\,km sun-synchronous) propagated with
SGP4~\cite{vallado2006sgp4} from a CelesTrak two-line element set~\cite{celestrak}, sampled every minute. At each
position $\mathbf r(t)$ we evaluate the geomagnetic field magnitude $|B(\mathbf r)|$ from IGRF-13~\cite{alken2021igrf}
and map it to a per-bit upset rate by a linear ramp:
\begin{equation}
\lambda(t)=\lambda_{\mathrm{bg}}+(\lambda_{\mathrm{SAA}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{bg}})\,
\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{B_{\mathrm{e}}-|B(\mathbf r(t))|}{B_{\mathrm{e}}-B_{\mathrm{c}}},0,1\right),
\label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
with edge $B_{\mathrm e}=20{,}000$\,nT and core $B_{\mathrm c}=18{,}000$\,nT. The number of upsets in a step of length
$\Delta$ is Poisson with mean $B\int_{t}^{t+\Delta}\lambda(\tau)\,d\tau$, integrated per minute.
Fig.~\ref{fig:orbit} shows one day of the track over the field.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\caption{One day of the Sentinel-2A ground track (SGP4) over IGRF field magnitude at altitude. Solid and dashed
contours mark the SAA core ($18{,}000$\,nT) and edge ($20{,}000$\,nT) between which the upset rate ramps.}
\label{fig:orbit}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Calibration.} We fix the ratio $\lambda_{\mathrm{SAA}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{bg}}=75.3$ and choose
$\lambda_{\mathrm{bg}}=7.89\times10^{-8}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{SAA}}=5.94\times10^{-6}$ upsets/bit/day so that the
orbit average over the real track ($79.9$ SAA-core-equivalent minutes per day) reproduces the Alsat-1 mean of
$4.04\times10^{-7}$~\cite{bentoutou2011edac}. About $82\%$ of upsets then occur inside the SAA. As a consistency check,
the calibrated rate predicts $358.1\times10^{6}\times4.04\times10^{-7}=145$ flips/day for ResNet-18 and $29.4$ for
MobileNetV2; the campaigns recorded $147.1\pm16.6$ and $29.1\pm0.5$.

\subsection{Placement and Multiplicity}
Each upset event is an SBU with probability $0.9855$, otherwise an MBU cluster of up to four adjacent bits. Events are
placed uniformly over stored bits: a block is chosen with probability proportional to its bit count, then a tensor,
an element and a bit. All 32 bit positions are eligible; restricting injection to the exponent would inflate severity.

\subsection{Paired Replay}
A single seeded injector samples the fault stream for the unprotected replica, recording
$(\text{block},\text{tensor},\text{index},\text{bit})$ tuples. Every other strategy runs on its own deep copy of the
model and \emph{replays the identical records}. Differences between strategies within a seed are therefore paired, and
we report paired differences with Student-$t$ intervals.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
A repair \emph{strategy} $\pi$ observes, at each step $t$, only the unlabeled incoming batch $X_t$ and its own model
state, and may rewrite any subset of blocks from the golden copy. Let $A_\pi(t)$ be the top-1 accuracy of its model on
a held-out labeled set after step $t$, $D_\pi(T)$ the bytes it moved and $E_\pi(T)$ the energy it spent over a mission
of $T$ steps. We seek strategies that are high in mean accuracy
\begin{equation}
\bar A_\pi=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_\pi(t)
\end{equation}
and low in $D_\pi(T)$ and $E_\pi(T)$, without labels or ground contact. No single strategy need dominate on all three;
we therefore compare strategies on the accuracy--traffic plane and report energy separately. Periodic scrubbing with
interval $h$ traces a one-parameter family $\{(\bar A_h,D_h)\}$, the frontier that any detection-based policy must beat.

\caption{\aegis{} repair loop. Hooks on each incoming batch feed the behavioral tiers T1 and T2, which propose
candidate blocks without labels. T3 recomputes CRC-32 over each candidate and the block upstream of the earliest

\aegis{} runs once per monitoring period (one hour in our experiments) on the batch of images the satellite has just
acquired. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} gives the overview and Algorithm~\ref{alg:aegis} the procedure.

\subsection{Design Evolution: From Aegis-v1 to v2}
The design was shaped by what did not work. A first prototype (Aegis-v1) borrowed from plasticity research: it
watched for ``dead'' low-firing neurons in a small multilayer perceptron, modeled faults as zeroed weights, and
revived neurons with noise or re-initialization in the spirit of ReDo~\cite{sokar2023redo} and continual
backpropagation~\cite{dohare2024plasticity}. A prior-art review showed three problems. First, the fault model was wrong:
SEUs in float32 weights create huge values, not dead units, so a dead-neuron monitor watches for the opposite symptom.
Second, re-initialization re-randomizes capacity but cannot recover a specific learned function without a training
signal, and an inference-only payload has neither gradients nor labels. Third, a flat MLP does not represent the
batch-normalized CNNs flown in practice. Aegis-v2 keeps the idea of a self-monitoring network but replaces each piece
with one grounded in the reliability literature: range and BN-statistic monitors in place of dead-neuron counts
(inspired by Ranger~\cite{chen2021ranger} and BLINK~\cite{blink2026}), checksums in place of heuristics (as in
RADAR~\cite{li2021radar}), and exact restore from a golden copy in place of revival, the operation scrubbing already
trusts~\cite{petersen2011see}.

\subsection{Blocks}
We partition $\theta$ into ordered \emph{blocks}, the unit of detection, restoration and accounting: for ResNet-18 the
stem, the eight residual blocks \texttt{layerX.Y} and the classifier (10 blocks, $38.7$\,kB to $18.9$\,MB); for
MobileNetV2 the 19 \texttt{features.N} stages and the classifier (20 blocks). A single function maps both parameter
keys and module names to block identifiers. This matters: an early version used a separate mapping for monitor alarms
with a fallback that silently sent every MobileNetV2 alarm to the stem.

\subsection{Tier 1: Activation Envelope}
During calibration on $n\ge100$ clean chips we record, for every convolution and linear layer $l$, the minimum $m_l$
and maximum $M_l$ of its output. The envelope is widened by a margin $\alpha=0.5$ of the range:
\begin{equation}
[\ell_l,u_l]=[m_l-\alpha(M_l-m_l),\;M_l+\alpha(M_l-m_l)].
\end{equation}
At run time layer $l$ raises an alarm if any output is NaN or $\pm\infty$ or falls outside $[\ell_l,u_l]$. Tier 1
catches exponent-MSB explosions immediately. We stress the choice of envelope: an envelope of mean $\pm k\sigma$ fires
on essentially every clean batch because activations are heavy-tailed, which silently degenerates the gate into
``restore everything'' (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).

\subsection{Tier 2: Batch-Norm Drift}
Each batch-norm layer stores running statistics $(\mu_c,\sigma^2_c)$ per channel $c$. For the input $x$ of BN layer $l$
on the current batch, with batch statistics $(\hat\mu_c,\hat\sigma_c)$ and $s_c=\sqrt{\sigma_c^2+\epsilon}$,
$\hat s_c=\sqrt{\hat\sigma_c^2+\epsilon}$, the drift score is
\begin{equation}
d_l=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\left(\frac{|\hat\mu_c-\mu_c|}{s_c}+\frac{|\hat s_c-s_c|}{s_c}\right),
\end{equation}
set to a sentinel value if any statistic is non-finite. The per-layer threshold is calibrated on clean batches of the
run-time batch size as $\tau_l=\max(3.5,\;2.5\max_{\text{clean}} d_l)$. Tier 2 is sensitive to mid-exponent and large
mantissa shifts that keep activations inside the T1 envelope.

\subsection{Tier 3: CRC Confirmation and the Upstream Rule}
Behavioral alarms say where to \emph{look}, not what is broken: an exponent flip in one block corrupts every
activation downstream, so T1 and T2 typically flag a cascade of blocks. Tier 3 resolves this exactly. At calibration
it stores the CRC-32 of each block's serialized parameters; at run time it recomputes the CRC of each candidate and
declares a block corrupted iff the checksums differ. Cascade victims pass and are not restored.

T1 hooks convolution outputs and T2 hooks BN \emph{inputs}, so a fault in a BN parameter at the end of block $k$, e.g.\
a sign flip on a running variance that produces NaN, first becomes visible at the hooks of block $k{+}1$. \aegis{}
therefore always adds the block immediately upstream of the earliest candidate to the T3 set. Without this rule a seed
in our campaign stayed at $10\%$ accuracy for the rest of the mission because the culprit, \texttt{layer3.0.bn2}, was
never checksummed.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\aegis{} step on an incoming batch}
\label{alg:aegis}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE model blocks $b_1,\dots,b_K$ in forward order; envelopes $[\ell_l,u_l]$; thresholds $\tau_l$; golden CRCs $g_k$; golden store $\mathcal G$
\ENSURE restored block set $R$
\STATE run forward pass on the batch with T1/T2 hooks attached
\STATE $C\leftarrow\{\mathrm{block}(l) : \text{T1 alarm at } l \lor d_l>\tau_l\}$
\IF{$C=\emptyset$}
  \RETURN $\emptyset$ \COMMENT{healthy: no checksum, no traffic}
\ENDIF
\STATE $k^\ast\leftarrow\min\{k : b_k\in C\}$;\; $C\leftarrow C\cup\{b_{k^\ast-1}\}$ \COMMENT{upstream rule}
\STATE $R\leftarrow\{b_k\in C : \mathrm{CRC32}(b_k)\neq g_k\}$
\FORALL{$b_k\in R$ in forward order}
  \STATE $b_k\leftarrow\mathrm{decompress}(\mathcal G[k])$ \COMMENT{bit-exact restore}
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $R$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Golden Store and Restore}
The golden copy holds one zstd-compressed~\cite{rfc8878} safetensors blob per block. Trained float32 weights compress
poorly ($44.77\to41.58$\,MB for ResNet-18, $9.08\to8.44$\,MB for MobileNetV2), so the store is essentially the model
size. Restores are bit-exact, so no batch-norm recalibration~\cite{shomron2020bnrecal} is needed; we keep it optional
and off, and charge one inference per buffered image when enabled. An INT8 variant (per-output-channel symmetric
quantization) shrinks the ResNet-18 store to $9.2$\,MB at a cost of $0.7$ points on 1,000 chips; its restores are lossy,
so restored blocks are re-baselined individually in T3. A pitfall: refreshing all golden checksums while any block
may be corrupt bakes the corruption into the reference and makes it permanently invisible; \aegis{} only ever
re-baselines blocks it has just restored.

\subsection{CRC Audit for Sub-Threshold Faults}
Behavioral detection cannot see a fault that keeps activations inside the envelope and statistics inside tolerance.
In one seed a single weight changed from $0.106$ to $1.7$, cost 17 points of accuracy, and raised no alarm. The
\emph{\aegis{} + CRC audit} variant therefore checksums every block on a fixed period (6\,h by default) and restores any
mismatch, independently of the behavioral gate. We report it alongside plain \aegis{}.

\subsection{Combining with Range Restriction}
Ranger-style clamping can run under \aegis{}: monitor hooks are registered before the clamp hooks so that T1 observes
raw activations, and the clamps limit damage in the interval before a restore. We evaluate this combination as
\emph{Ranger + \aegis{}}.

\subsection{Cost Model}
\label{sec:costmodel}
Every repairing strategy is charged for the bytes it moves and the energy that costs. A restore of block $k$ with
compressed size $c_k$ and raw size $r_k$ costs
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{restore}}(k)=8\,c_k\,e_{\mathrm{flash}}+8\,r_k\,e_{\mathrm{DRAM,w}},
\end{equation}
with $e_{\mathrm{flash}}=15$\,pJ/bit and $e_{\mathrm{DRAM,w}}=25$\,pJ/bit. A T3 checksum of block $k$ costs
$8\,r_k\,e_{\mathrm{DRAM,r}}$ with $e_{\mathrm{DRAM,r}}=20$\,pJ/bit. The T1/T2 hooks cost
$e_{\mathrm{op}}=6.9$\,pJ per elementwise operation (four passes over each convolution/linear output, three over each BN
input), obtained by dividing a $12.5$\,mJ ResNet-18 inference on a Jetson-class device by its $1.82$\,G MACs; this
treats an elementwise pass as one MAC and is likely pessimistic. Monitoring is charged only on the monitored incoming
batch, not on evaluation passes. The ground uplink receives at 1\,Mbps during four 480\,s contacts per day ($\approx60$\,MB
per pass, as reported for Chaohu-1~\cite{rednet2024}) at $500$\,pJ/bit. Ranger and BLINK move no bytes and their
per-inference compute is not charged. All constants are assumptions of order-of-magnitude fidelity
(Table~\ref{tab:constants}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Data and splits.} EuroSAT RGB chips are bicubically upsampled to $224\times224$ and normalized with EuroSAT
channel statistics; logits follow the checkpoint's label order. Calibration (10 chips per class, 100 total) and
labeled evaluation (15 per class, 150 total) sets are disjoint. Monitoring operates on a rotating window of 16
unlabeled chips per step; labels are used only to measure accuracy.

\textbf{Models.} ResNet-18 uses a public EuroSAT checkpoint (\texttt{cm93/resnet18-eurosat}) with $89.3\%$ accuracy on
our evaluation set ($92.8\%$ on a 1,000-chip check); its training split is not published, so its clean accuracy may be
optimistic, though relative degradation is unaffected. To remove that caveat on a second architecture we fine-tuned an
ImageNet-initialized~\cite{deng2009imagenet} MobileNetV2 for three epochs (AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} with a
one-cycle schedule~\cite{smith2019superconvergence}, batch 64, learning rate $10^{-3}$, flip and $90^\circ$-rotation
augmentation) on 26,035 chips, holding out the 965 chips used by every campaign split; the simulator refuses any split
not contained in the held-out set. It reaches $99.3\%$ on the evaluation set.

\textbf{Mission.} Each campaign simulates three days in 72 one-hour steps for three radiation seeds, with the
Sentinel-2A track and the rate model of Section~\ref{sec:faultmodel}. Per step the simulator injects the step's
upsets, replays them on every replica, runs each strategy's repair logic, and evaluates every replica on the 150
labeled chips.

\textbf{Strategies.} (1)~\emph{Unmitigated}. (2)~\emph{Ranger clipping}: activations clamped to profiled bounds with a
$0.1$ margin~\cite{chen2021ranger}. (3)~\emph{BLINK re-centering}: anomalous BN inputs renormalized to reference
statistics (threshold 3.0)~\cite{blink2026}. (4)~\emph{Periodic scrubbing}: full restore every 12\,h, plus a sweep at
1, 3, 6 and 24\,h. (5)~\emph{Ground uplink}: CRC-confirmed blocks queued and reloaded only during contacts, rate-limited
by the link. (6)~\emph{\aegis{}}. (7)~\emph{\aegis{} + CRC audit} (6\,h). (8)~\emph{Ranger + \aegis{}}.

\textbf{Metrics.} The primary metric is \emph{mean accuracy}, the time average of top-1 accuracy over all steps, which
reflects the fraction of the mission spent producing correct answers. We also report final accuracy (a single noisy
snapshot), repair traffic in MB/day, and modeled energy in mJ/day. Intervals are 95\% Student-$t$ over the three seeds
($t_{0.975,2}=4.30$), so they are wide; paired differences use the same fault stream per seed. The \emph{bandwidth
verdict} asks whether any scrubbing interval dominates a strategy (higher accuracy at no more traffic) and how much
traffic scrubbing needs to match its accuracy, interpolating linearly between swept intervals.

\textbf{Implementation.} The simulator is written in PyTorch. The final campaigns ran on an NVIDIA T4 (about 200\,s per
seed for ResNet-18 with 12 model replicas, 225\,s for MobileNetV2). The repository includes 51 unit and regression
tests, including regression tests for the evaluation pitfalls of Section~\ref{sec:lessons}.

\subsection{Accuracy Under Accumulating Upsets}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy} give the main results. On ResNet-18, with $147$ upsets per day and
$4.9$ on the exponent MSB, the unprotected model is at chance for almost the entire mission (mean $14.0\%$, final
$10.0\%$ in every seed). Range clipping and BN re-centering slow the collapse but cannot reverse it, because the
corrupted weights stay in memory and accumulate (means $38.5\%$ and $34.0\%$, ending at $27.1\%$ and $13.6\%$). Twelve-hour
scrubbing returns the model to clean accuracy at each reload but spends most of each interval broken (mean $37.0\%$).
The ground uplink repairs only during contacts (mean $66.2\%$). \aegis{} holds $87.4\%$ against a clean $89.3\%$; with the
CRC audit, $88.9\%$ with a final accuracy equal to clean in every seed.

The gap to plain \aegis{} comes from a single event: at hour 49 of seed~1 a sub-threshold weight fault
($0.106\to1.7$) removed 17 points without raising any alarm and persisted to the end of the mission. The audit closes it within six hours at a cost of
$49\%$ more traffic and $40\%$ more energy; the paired difference ($-1.5\pm6.6$ points) is inside the interval.
Combining Ranger with \aegis{} gives identical accuracy here and $24\%$ less traffic. Its restore events are as
frequent (63 versus 62), so the saving comes from restoring fewer or smaller blocks per event; we have not isolated
the mechanism.

MobileNetV2 stores five times fewer bits and is hit five times less often (29 upsets/day). Its ReLU6 activations are
bounded by construction, which plausibly explains why range clipping is far more effective on it ($98.8\%$) than on
ResNet-18. All three \aegis{} variants hold the clean $99.3\%$ in every seed.

\subsection{Accuracy per Byte: the Bandwidth Verdict}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_pareto.pdf}
\caption{Mean accuracy versus repair traffic. The orange curve sweeps the scrubbing interval from 24\,h to 1\,h;
strategies that move no bytes are drawn at $0.5$\,MB/day. All \aegis{} variants lie above and to the left of the
scrubbing frontier on both architectures.}
\label{fig:pareto}
\end{figure*}

Scrubbing has one knob, the interval, which trades traffic for accuracy along the curve of Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}. No
interval dominates any \aegis{} variant on either architecture. Interpolating the frontier, scrubbing needs about
$889$\,MB/day to reach \aegis{}'s $87.4\%$ on ResNet-18, $4.7\times$ the $188$\,MB/day \aegis{} moves; $975$\,MB/day to
match the audit variant ($3.5\times$); and the combination with Ranger reaches the same accuracy at $6.2\times$ less
than the matching scrub. On MobileNetV2 the ratio is about $60\times$, mostly because \aegis{} moves almost nothing
($3.4$\,MB/day, nine restore events over all seeds) while the matching 1-hour scrub rewrites the model 24 times a day.

Why is \aegis{}'s ResNet-18 traffic not smaller still? Because the blocks that get hit are the blocks that hold the
bits. Over the three seeds \aegis{} performed 62 restore events covering 162 block restores, of which $92\%$ were the
four \texttt{layer3}/\texttt{layer4} blocks that hold $94\%$ of the parameters ($3.7$ to $18.9$\,MB each; restore counts
57, 40, 39 and 13). Layer selectivity saves the most when damage is confined to small blocks; for a model whose
parameters concentrate at the end, a restore is a large fraction of a full scrub, and the saving comes mainly from
\emph{not} restoring when nothing is wrong.

\subsection{Energy: a Negative Result}
\label{sec:costs}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_costs.pdf}
\caption{Repair traffic and modeled energy per day. Solid: ResNet-18; hatched: MobileNetV2. \aegis{}'s energy is
dominated by always-on monitoring, not by restores.}
\label{fig:costs}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:costs} separates the two costs. Under our energy model \aegis{} spends $144$\,mJ/day on ResNet-18 and
$126$\,mJ/day on MobileNetV2, versus $27.9$ and $5.7$\,mJ/day for 12-hour scrubbing: $5.2\times$ and $22\times$ more.
Monitoring dominates. The T1/T2 hooks alone cost $46$\,mJ/day (about $1.9$\,mJ per 16-image ResNet-18 batch, ten
full-model reload equivalents of memory energy over three days), and together with T3 checksum reads monitoring is
$56\%$ of \aegis{}'s energy on ResNet-18 and $98\%$ on MobileNetV2, whose many narrow layers make elementwise hooks
relatively expensive. Our first prototype claimed a $98.9\%$ energy saving because it did not charge the monitor; that
claim was wrong. With the monitor charged, \aegis{} is an energy \emph{cost}, and our honest claim is about bandwidth
and availability. Two caveats cut in its favor: the $6.9$\,pJ per elementwise operation is likely pessimistic for
fused hardware reductions, and monitoring could run on every $n$-th batch, trading detection latency for energy
linearly. The ground uplink is the most expensive option by far ($635$\,mJ/day on ResNet-18) because radio reception
costs an order of magnitude more per bit than memory.

\subsection{Where Faults Land and What Gets Restored}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_faults.pdf}
\caption{Upsets per one-hour step for ResNet-18 seed~1 against SAA-core minutes in the step. Upsets cluster in the
SAA passes; exponent-MSB hits (triangles) arrive about five times a day; dashed lines mark steps where \aegis{}

\label{fig:faults}

Fig.~\ref{fig:faults} shows the arrival process for one seed. Upsets bunch into the consecutive daily SAA passes, and
quiet hours alternate with bursts of 20--30 flips. Of the 1,324 ResNet-18 upsets over three seeds, $72.5\%$ landed in

uniform placement. Restores coincide with exponent-MSB hits and with other damaging hits (sign flips on BN variances,
high exponent bits), and \aegis{} is silent through the many steps whose upsets touch only mantissas.

\subsection{Transient Faults Do Not Trigger Restores}
\label{sec:transient}
A behavioral monitor alone cannot distinguish a transient glitch in an activation buffer from corrupted weights. We
injected exponent-MSB flips into activations, not weights, in 40 single-batch trials on ResNet-18. Tier 1 or 2 raised
an alarm in $27.5\%$ of trials; Tier 3 found no checksum mismatch in any, so the spurious-restore rate was $0\%$ and no
bytes were moved. Mean accuracy over the trials changed from $89.7\%$ clean to $88.6\%$ with the transient fault (the
glitch affects one batch only), and to $89.7\%$ with range clipping. This is the second purpose of the CRC gate: beyond
localizing cascades, it makes repair decisions robust to faults that need no repair.

\section{Lessons: Pitfalls That Invalidated Results}
\label{sec:lessons}
Each of the following silently produced a plausible but wrong result during this work. We list them because each is
easy to reproduce elsewhere; the fixes are covered by regression tests in the repository.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Synthetic inputs.} Evaluating on Gaussian-noise tensors gave accuracy numbers with no meaning; our
  first headline (``98.9\% energy savings'') came from random images, an always-firing gate and an uncharged monitor.
  \item \textbf{Input contract.} The checkpoint expects $224$\,px bicubic-upsampled chips with dataset statistics and its
  own label order. $64$\,px input gives $\approx18\%$; alphabetical labels give chance.
  \item \textbf{Envelope statistics.} A mean$\pm k\sigma$ activation envelope fires on every clean batch because
  activations are heavy-tailed; the ``detection-gated'' method then restores on every step. Bounds must be observed
  extrema plus margin over $\ge100$ calibration chips.
  \item \textbf{Calibration leakage.} Calibration and evaluation chips must be disjoint; and a model trained by the
  authors must exclude both (enforced for MobileNetV2).
  \item \textbf{Poisoned references.} Re-computing golden checksums while a block may be corrupt makes that corruption
  undetectable forever.
  \item \textbf{Hook placement.} T1/T2 observe a BN-parameter fault one block downstream; without the upstream rule the
  culprit is never checksummed.
  \item \textbf{Name mapping.} Two code paths mapping layer names to blocks diverged for MobileNetV2 and sent every alarm
  to the stem.
  \item \textbf{Fault field.} Restricting injection to exponent bits raises the rate of exponent-MSB hits fourfold relative
  to uniform placement; any such choice must be disclosed.
  \item \textbf{Uncharged costs.} Omitting monitor compute, CRC reads or recalibration inferences turns an energy cost
  into an apparent saving.
\end{enumerate}

% =====================================================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Why gating works.} Most upsets are harmless: $72\%$ hit mantissas and change a weight by less than a factor of
two. A blind scrubber pays for all of them; an in-place mitigator pays for none of them but cannot undo the rare
catastrophic one. \aegis{} pays only when behavior \emph{and} checksums agree that something is broken, which is what
lets it sit above the scrubbing frontier.

\textbf{When it does not.} The behavioral gate has a blind spot, faults large enough to matter but too small to move
activations past calibrated bounds. The audit closes it at a cost that scales with model size and audit frequency; a
mission could schedule audits after SAA exits, when new faults are most likely, instead of on a fixed clock. Gating
also cannot help when monitoring itself dominates energy (MobileNetV2), and it cannot repair the golden copy, whose
integrity rests on rad-tolerant storage and its own ECC.

\textbf{Deployment.} The golden copy fits comfortably in MRAM or rad-hard NOR for models of this size, or in $9.2$\,MB
with INT8 at a small accuracy cost. T3 needs only a CRC engine and a table of 32-bit references; T1/T2 need reductions
over activations that many accelerators can fuse into the layers that produce them. The method is complementary to
ECC: with ECC on the working memory, \aegis{} would act on the residue of uncorrectable and logic-induced errors.

% =====================================================================================================
\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:validity}
\textbf{Radiation model.} Rates are transferred from Alsat-1 (686\,km, 2002-era SRAM) to Sentinel-2A at 786\,km and to
modern memory, whose sensitivity can differ by orders of magnitude. The SAA-to-background ratio is assumed, the IGRF
ramp is a proxy for a trapped-proton flux model such as AP8/AE9 combined with device cross-sections, solar-cycle
variation is not modeled, and faults are injected only into parameters, not into control logic or activation buffers,
which beam studies show can dominate~\cite{dossantos2023neutron}.
\textbf{Statistics.} Three seeds and 150 evaluation chips give wide intervals; one chip is $0.67$ points. The MobileNetV2
paired differences are mostly not significant.
\textbf{Energy.} All energy constants are analytic proxies, not measurements on flight hardware, and Ranger/BLINK
compute is not charged, which flatters those baselines.
\textbf{Models.} The ResNet-18 checkpoint's training split is unknown; MobileNetV2 has a verified held-out split.
\textbf{Mission length.} Three days captures several SAA cycles and dozens of exponent-MSB hits, but not long-term
effects such as total ionizing dose.
\textbf{Literature.} Several closely related works are 2026 preprints whose results we cite as reported.

% =====================================================================================================
\section{Future Work}
Natural extensions are (i)~orbit-aware scheduling of audits and monitoring duty cycle around SAA passes, which directly
targets both the blind spot and the energy cost; (ii)~a physics-based environment (AP8/AE9 with device cross-sections
via SPENVIS or CREME96); (iii)~measured energy and latency on a Jetson- or NPU-class board; (iv)~activation and logic
faults alongside weight faults; (v)~vision transformers and larger EO tasks such as segmentation; and (vi)~class-level
analysis of which semantic confusions (e.g.\ River vs.\ Highway) radiation induces, for which our simulator already
records per-class predictions.

% =====================================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
Onboard classifiers in LEO accumulate silent, sometimes catastrophic weight corruption. We showed that combining cheap
behavioral signals with exact checksums lets a satellite repair only what is broken, when it is broken: on an
orbit-calibrated, paired simulation of a Sentinel-2A mission, \aegis{} kept a ResNet-18 within two points of clean
accuracy where 12-hour scrubbing, range clipping, re-centering and ground reload lost 23 to 55 points, and it did so with several
times less repair traffic than the scrubbing interval that would match it. The same analysis also showed what it does
not do: with honest accounting, always-on monitoring costs more energy than a slow scrub. We hope the simulator, the
pitfalls and the results help the next design start from realistic faults and complete budgets.
